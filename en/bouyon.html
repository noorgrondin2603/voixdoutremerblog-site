Bouyon, From One Island to Another: The History of a Caribbean Music in Motion

This is an English translation (AI-assisted, reviewed by the author) of an article originally published in French on Sillages Sonores. Read the original in French →

13 min read · By Noor-Sharina Grondin — Voix d'Outre-Mer · Originally published June 19, 2026

Some music crosses borders without a passport. Bouyon is one of them. Born in Dominica in the decades following Caribbean independence, it took twenty years to reach Guadeloupean parties, and ten more to produce its own scene, its own artists, its own controversies there. Today, the term "bouyon gwada" has become established in Antillean musical vocabulary as the lexical trace of a profound cultural transformation. How did a music from an island of 70,000 people become the reference genre of Guadeloupean nightlife? This is the story I want to tell here.

I. Roseau, the 1980s: When Dominica Invents Its Own Musical Language

To understand what bouyon became in Guadeloupe, we must first understand what it was before it left. Bouyon is a deeply Dominican genre. It was born in the capital, Roseau, carried by a founding group: WCK (Windward Caribbean Kulture)1 and the Phillip Brothers. Its musical genealogy is Caribbean in the broadest sense of the term. DJ Kévy, a Guadeloupean beatmaker and composer interviewed as part of my doctoral research, describes it as "a mix — kadans, lypso, jump up, bouyon of zouk and calypso."

Naomi Green, director of a booking agency working with Dominican artists, refines this lineage: bouyon descends from calypso kadans, in which "people sang about the political situation back home." The original instrumentation reflects this cultural anchoring: piano, live percussion, conch shell. A sound that is at once carnal, organic, and political. Bouyon proper was born with a BPM between 150 and 190.

In its earliest forms, the genre was called Jump Pop or Jump Up. Triple Kay inherited it and gave it its definitive name at the turn of the 2000s.

It is festive, danceable, often bawdy. But it also carries a social memory. DJ Swafet, a Guadeloupean composer with a rare, precise knowledge of the Dominican repertoire, puts it well: "MFR Band — State Of Emergency2: it's a track that describes the island's political, social, and economic situation, but the instrumental is danceable."

This paradox of a joyful instrumental carrying a heavy text is a Dominican signature. In Guadeloupe, the genre would first take root through its festive dimension.

II. The Channel, the Bodies, the Parties: How Bouyon Arrives in Guadeloupe

There is no founding act, no official date, no press release. Bouyon arrived in Guadeloupe the way many things arrive in the islands: through people who move. The first articles attacking bouyon in Guadeloupe date from 2010.

Dominican artists settled in Guadeloupe over the course of the 2000s. Among them, Asa Bantan and Suppa. Suppa3, a member of the "Vadore Concept" collective, had built the Gaza Crew around him, a collective of bouyon artists. They carried their music with them in their luggage. They played at parties, celebrations, carnival spaces where the usual musical norms were temporarily suspended. It was in these interstices that bouyon began to circulate. It was with Tèt brilé, the first track to infiltrate Guadeloupean parties, that the style made its entrance. It immediately won over the archipelago's youth. Another turning point: the creation of the Gaza Girls in 2010. "The girls were saying4 'ka ou vlé fess ka fann,' they were talking about the way men spoke to them," explains Vadore. But according to journalist Christophe Courtois, "the young people took it as an invitation. An invitation that singer Miky Ding La would take literally with his track Biten a koukoune woz."

But the history of a music is rarely the history of a single, spontaneous migration. It is also the history of those who take the economic risk of making it exist. Cédric Rabotteur, known as Vadore, is a producer and manager in Pointe-à-Pitre. He grew up in Vieux Maurabime, in Les Abymes, in what he himself describes as "a ghetto, a poor, unsanitary neighborhood with assaults, murders, police raids." At the turn of the 2010s, he made a simple observation: the party market was saturated. Dancehall, zouk, and kompa were sharing a space that hadn't grown. He went to Dominica for a month, met the bands, the artists, the composers. He came back to Guadeloupe and spent a year building the logistical conditions to bring them to play. The first bouyon parties in Guadeloupe were the result of this work. Vadore himself sums up the stakes:

"It's important to know where bouyon comes from, how it arrived in Guadeloupe, because there are no records, no history written on the subject."

III. 2008–2013: The First Guadeloupean Generation

Starting in 2008, something changes. Guadeloupean artists take hold of the bouyon rhythm to lay their own words over it. DJ Swafet identifies them as the "precursors of Guadeloupean bouyon": Yellow Gaza and Miky Ding La. Around them forms the Gaza Crew, centered in Pointe-à-Pitre. The tracks produced in this period are underground, recorded with very limited means, played at parties with no commercial ambition. Miky Ding La puts it bluntly:

"They were dirty, perverted sounds, they weren't commercial sounds, so it was underground."
"We can't talk about cultural appropriation. [...] There was no copying in Bouyon, but a cultural mix. There was a fusion between the two. There's really this idea of an outstretched hand, of working together." — Naomi Green

This nascent scene also attracted the first female artists. Gaza Girl Crew, Yellow Gaza, Ghetto Gaza: three all-female collectives took the mic over the bouyon rhythm, in a register Vadore describes as an "unhinged" sexuality, at a time when "that wasn't common." They reclaimed the raw character of bouyon by describing the sex act from their own point of view as women, reversing the male/female balance of power in their music. The result was founding hits like Ka ou Vlé for the Gaza Girls Crew or Volé Nom a Moun for Yellow Gaza.5 These women were pioneers. They would not be spared controversy.

In 2012, controversy erupted.6 Violent incidents at parties were linked to the playing of bouyon. The prefect attempted a ban.7 Rumors circulated about a possible ban.8 Some DJs lost their jobs. France-Antilles ran the headline "Bouyon, the height of provocation?" The tone says it all: this isn't a music being analyzed, it's a music being judged.

What the corpus reveals is that bouyon was then read by institutions as a dangerous music: obscene lyrics, uncontrollable energy, unruly youth. This is not the first time in the history of Caribbean popular music that a genre born in the working classes has run into this kind of reading. Zouk went through its own version of this trial. So did Jamaican dancehall. Bouyon is not an exception. It is a repetition.

In September 2012, a rumor announcing a supposed ban on bouyon circulated on social media and in some local press.9

A persistent rumor that fed the bouyon myth. After conducting research for this article, I found no source confirming this ban. These rumors remain speculative. Nevertheless, this episode testifies to the recurring controversies surrounding bouyon and its high visibility in Guadeloupean public space. They feed into the genre's legend.

Taking the Mic: Women, Bodies, and Freedom in Bouyon

What these women do over the bouyon rhythm is precise: they turn the gaze around. Where male lyrics describe the female body as an object of desire or conquest, they describe their own as a subject. They speak of their pleasure, their conditions, what they want and what they refuse. Vadore sums it up this way: they "promote an 'unhinged' sexuality" — and that "wasn't common."

Ten years later, Yu Meï carries this movement forward. A singer and songwriter who is her own art director down to her fingertips, she entered bouyon in 2020 with Eat Up, a freestyle improvised over a DJ Swafet instrumental. The track became an instant hit. She didn't expect it to take off — and perhaps it worked precisely because it wasn't calculated.

What she says about bouyon deserves to be quoted directly:

"This genre allows women, in particular, to free their speech." And further: "Bouyon is liberating, there's a leadership and feminist side to it (it depends on the artist) that speaks to a real need for freedom of speech. If women talk about these subjects, it's because there's a need."

She locates this need beyond music. Women's speech in bouyon is, in her view, "a bit of a reshaping of society." This discourse "can bother men, in the sense that they have to get used to a new way of operating, to listening to women and their issues." What she articulates here, in her own words, resembles what gender scholars call a disruption of norms — except that she simply says: "it's above all a need for justice."

This speech comes at a price. Yu Meï documents, without flinching, what she experiences: cyberbullying, body-shaming, criticism for being too "emancipated." From men and women, mostly over thirty. "The fact that she's a woman talking about sexuality is frowned upon," she says. And adds: "it has a socio-political dimension." The punishment isn't just moral — it's structural. It reveals what Antillean society still accepts and what it still punishes.

But the pressure doesn't silence her. Yu Meï keeps going. And she isn't alone. Holly G, Shakina, Miimii KDS, Fallon: a new generation of women is settling into bouyon without asking permission. Bouyon, Yu Meï notes, "is gaining influence, women are speaking up more and more." This isn't a footnote in the genre's history. It's one of its most alive chapters.

IV. 2014–2025: The Long March Toward Legitimacy

The 2012 boycott did not kill bouyon. It hardened it, forced it to structure itself. In the years that followed, the scene consolidated quietly. The gap between Dominican bouyon and "bouyon gwada" widened. Dominicans themselves began using this term to designate the Guadeloupean version of the genre.

In Dominica, bouyon is fully part of the national musical landscape. This recognition was notably marked in 2015 by its featured place at the Creole Music Festival and by the creation of Bouyon Day, an annual day dedicated to celebrating and promoting the genre.10

The distinction is musical: where Dominican bouyon relies on live instrumentation, bouyon gwada is computer-produced. It is also textual: Guadeloupean freestyle moves away from the Dominican political register to focus on partying, sexuality, ego, the neighborhood.

The commercial turning point came in 2019. The duo TBTS and LeStef produced 50/50, the first bouyon track to reach a million streams. A TikTok trend followed. The genre entered the national musical landscape. In 2021, Gaza Gyal's track marks, for Vadore, the first true "Guadeloupean bouyon" in the full sense of the term. In September of the same year, Santorini by Edday and TBTS united the Caribbean and mainland France in a single trend. Bouyon stopped being a local music to become a regional, then a mainland French, one.

A shift occurred in 2022, documented by RCI Guadeloupe.11 While bouyon was in full "decline," a new generation took over. As Sappy, founder of Voodoo Studio, sums it up: "Bouyon is a mix of carnival music and club music."

Voodoo Studio, Holly G, Boutcha Bwa, 1T1, Théomaa, Aknose, Softee, Shakina, TiiTii NBA, Miimii KDS… These are artists aged 18 to 25 who master the codes of social media as much as those of the scene. 1T1 owns it: he seeks to "push the boundaries" of the genre while making "clean bouyon." His EP Chord blends rap and bouyon, reggae and bouyon, afrobeat and bouyon.12 The "New bouyon wave" is proclaimed.13 Since the early 2020s, Martinique has begun taking a serious interest.14 So has mainland France, though the genre remains poorly understood there in the media.15,16

In 2025, Mr Ridge, an artist of Dominican origin, was named "Bouyon Artist of the Year" for the second consecutive time at the Caribbean Music Awards, proving that bouyon is not just a passing trend, but a solid movement and a central element of Dominican culture.17 He is recognized for his provocative lyrics and innovative productions.

V. What This History Says About the Caribbean

There is one last question this history forces us to ask. That of debt. Naomi Green puts it precisely: "All of Guadeloupe's biggest hits are inspired by the beats of Dominican producers like Ridge."

Dominica has no equivalent of a performance rights organization (SACEM). It lacks the legal structures that would allow its artists to receive the royalties their creative work entitles them to. Naomi Green speaks of a "real cultural and musical injustice." Vadore, for his part, refers in certain cases to a form of cultural appropriation, particularly when artists adopt the bouyon rhythm without naming its origin.

These tensions are not settled. The corpus I have built documents them but does not resolve them. What is certain is that the history of bouyon in Guadeloupe is also the history of a power relationship between two unequally endowed Caribbean islands: one a French territory, with access to French cultural institutions; the other an independent, English-speaking state, left outside the structures that protect and compensate creative work. This inequality is not musical. It is geopolitical.

Bouyon, in this sense, is far more than a musical genre. It is a revealer of the asymmetries that structure the contemporary Caribbean.
