Speakers on the Move: How Sound System Culture Traveled from Jamaica to the Caribbean

This is an English translation (AI-assisted, reviewed by the author) of an article originally published in French on Sillages Sonores. Read the original in French →

18 min read · Originally published on Sillages Sonores

You first recognize a sound system by its speakers. Yet the story doesn't fit inside a wall of loudspeakers. The term also designates a crew, a way of organizing a party, a space of learning, and a culture of circulation. Born in postwar urban Jamaica, the sound system travels with migration. It transforms in London, in Paris, in Marseille, in Martinique or in Guadeloupe. At every stop, it keeps something of Kingston while becoming something else.

How did a technology meant to amplify records become one of the most fertile engines in the history of Caribbean and diasporic popular music? To understand this, we have to follow the speakers — but also the people, the places, and the words that travel with them.

1. Before the Culture, a Technique: Amplifying Differently

The history of the sound system begins with a very concrete problem: how do you make recorded music heard with a power and quality able to rival the experience of a live show? In 1940s Jamaica, sound equipment technology was still expensive and hard to access. Radio repairmen, electronics technicians, and tinkerers experimented with their own solutions.

One testimony is especially valuable. In 2017, the Philharmonie de Paris published an interview with Hedley Jones, a musician, radio repairman, and former Royal Air Force radar engineer. Jones recounts that after returning from the Second World War, he opened Hedley Jones' Radio Service in Kingston. In 1947 he developed an amplifier capable of separately rendering bass, mid, and treble. At first, he used it in his shop to let customers listen to records.

According to his account, Tom Wong, the owner of a neighboring business, heard the difference. Wong was already running small parties with modest sound equipment. He asked Jones to build him a comparable amplifier. It was this system that Tom Wong would later call a "sound system." Jones's apprentice, Fred Sanford, gave him the nickname Tom the Great Sebastian. Jones went on to build other amplifiers, notably for Duke Reid. Fred Sanford, in turn, continued this activity and helped spread this equipment.

This account should not be read as the solitary invention, in a single day, of an entire culture. Rather, it offers access to one of the technical links in its emergence. The work of Norman Stolzoff and Julian Henriques shows that sound system culture took shape within a broader whole: the urbanization of Kingston, the rise of recorded music, working-class sociability, entrepreneurship, and competition between operators. But Hedley Jones's story reminds us of something essential: before being an aesthetic, the sound system is also a culture of ingenuity.

2. Kingston: When the Record Becomes an Event

By the late 1940s, and especially through the 1950s, sound systems multiplied in Kingston. Operators set up their speakers in yards, halls, open spaces, or "lawns." People came to dance to recorded music. This choice was also economic: hiring a live band was expensive. A sound system and a record collection made it possible to get a large crowd dancing in a different way.

The repertoire at the time drew heavily on American rhythm and blues. Sound owners sought out novelties from the United States and tried to protect their finds. Tom the Great Sebastian, Arthur "Duke" Reid with his Trojan Sound, Clement "Coxsone" Dodd with Downbeat, and Vincent "King" Edwards became central figures in a highly competitive market.

Competition played out on several fronts at once. A powerful sound was necessary, but not sufficient. A selection of music capable of surprising the crowd was needed too. Owning a track your rival didn't have became an advantage. The selector — the person who chose and sequenced the records — thus acquired a strategic role. The audience, for its part, did not come to hear a simple playlist. It came to judge a collective performance.

It is also in this context that we must understand the Jamaican word "dance." It does not only mean the act of dancing. It can also refer to the evening itself, the event organized around the sound system. This nuance helps us grasp why the sound system is at once equipment, crew, and social situation.

3. From Importing to Producing: When Sounds Drove Jamaican Music

By the late 1950s, the American musical landscape was changing. Rock'n'roll was taking over on a massive scale, and part of African American rhythm and blues was shifting in sound and market. For Jamaican operators, the problem was not just that records were becoming "hard to get." It was also that the American repertoire was answering local audiences' expectations less and less well, even as competition between sounds still demanded novelty.

The solution was decisive: produce locally. Sound system owners such as Coxsone Dodd, Duke Reid, and King Edwards hired Jamaican musicians and financed recording sessions. Dependence on imports declined. Out of this new ecosystem grew ska, then rocksteady, then reggae.

The sound system then became a laboratory. Tracks were tested directly in front of an audience. An enthusiastic reaction could confirm that a track worked. A less effective selection could be dropped. The street, the hall, and the studio answered one another.

Deejays, Versions, and Dubplates

The Jamaican deejay is not exactly the same thing as a DJ in the everyday French sense. In sound system culture, the selector chooses the records. The deejay takes the microphone. He talks, chants, improvises, comments on the night, and "toasts" over instrumental versions. Count Machuki, King Stitt, and Sir Lord Comic are among the pioneers. U-Roy later helped transform this practice into a recorded form. His success at the turn of the 1970s shows that a voice born in the sound system could conquer the record itself.

This same environment fostered the proliferation of "versions." A recording could exist with vocals, without vocals, remixed, rearranged, or handed to another performer. The episode of Ruddy Redwood and the Paragons' "On the Beach," often told as a founding moment for the instrumental version, illustrates this logic. Dubplates further intensified competition: these were exclusive pressings, sometimes recorded specifically for one sound. In a clash, having a track no one else could find could make all the difference.

From this point on, the sound system no longer simply broadcast Jamaican music. It helped produce the very conditions for its renewal. The need for "fresh," for novelty, became one of the driving forces of the scene.

4. London: The Speakers Travel with Migration

After the Second World War, Caribbean migration to the United Kingdom carried with it records, party practices, and know-how. The London Museum presents Duke Vin — Vincent Forbes — as the first Jamaican sound system operator established in Britain, starting in 1955. He played notably at house parties around Ladbroke Grove.

In 1950s and 1960s Britain, these parties were far from trivial. Black people faced racism in housing, in employment, and in many social venues. Private homes, shebeens, and "blues dances" thus became spaces where people could listen to their own music, dance, and come together.

The Hackney Museum holds very telling accounts. Blues parties are described as paid parties held in houses, often lasting all night. A sound would move its equipment into the home. Admission was charged, drinks were sold, and for a few hours a space was created where the community could reconnect.

Landmark Venues

West London, around Notting Hill and Ladbroke Grove, became a major territory in this history. Duke Vin and Count Suckle played there. To the east, Hackney and Dalston also had a strong sound system culture. The Four Aces Club, in Dalston, hosted, for instance, Count Shelly and clash nights. Admiral Ken is associated with Hackney house parties and the Four Aces. Duke Vin also played at clubs in central London, such as the Flamingo and the Marquee.

This geography says something about the displacement underway. A sound system could be born in a house, move to a club, return to the street, then become a cultural emblem of the city. It also contributed to the emergence of specifically British forms: London dub, lovers rock, and, more broadly, a bass culture that would go on to influence jungle, drum and bass, UK garage, and grime.

5. Notting Hill Carnival: From the Margins to a Cultural Institution

The Notting Hill Carnival lets us see this transformation on a large scale. Held every year in London around the last weekend of August and the Bank Holiday, it grew out of the history of Caribbean communities settled in the British capital. Its development is set against the backdrop of the 1958 Notting Hill race riots, Claudia Jones's organizing, the Caribbean parties held in the late 1950s, and then the street festival launched in 1966 by Rhaune Laslett.

At first, traditions linked to the Trinidad and Tobago carnival held a central place: steelpan, calypso, mas, and costumes. But London also had a Caribbean youth who listened heavily to reggae and dub. In 1973, Leslie Palmer officially brought static sound systems into the carnival. The London Museum recalls that he considered them "the voice of the youth."

The shift was major. The sound system left private or semi-clandestine space to enter a street event that would become emblematic of London. Today, the carnival's official website still lists more than thirty static sound systems. They are not a secondary backdrop. They are one of the historic disciplines of the Notting Hill Carnival.

This permanence explains the carnival's current importance. It is at once a popular celebration, a space of transmission, a memory of Caribbean migration, and a contemporary stage. Mas, steelbands, soca, reggae, dub, and many strands of Black British music coexist there. The carnival thus shows that a diasporic tradition can become institutionalized without ceasing to evolve.

6. Mainland France: Paris, Marseille, and the Making of Local Scenes

Mainland France marks another important stage. Reggae was already circulating there in the 1970s, but records long remained tied to specialty shops and import networks. The Philharmonie de Paris documents sound-system-type experiments as early as 1979, in basements, squats, or an African community center (foyer).

In Paris, the five evenings of the Sound des Panoyaux in 1983 became a landmark. In the years that followed, sound systems served as nurseries for a generation of French reggae and ragga artists. Saï Saï, Daddy Yod, Nuttea, Princess Erika, Puppa Leslie, and Tonton David are all associated with this environment. Here too, the sound system functioned as a scene before it became an industry.

Marseille: Understanding the Sound System from Afar

In Marseille, Jean-Christophe Sevin shows a different trajectory. The free radio stations of the early 1980s served as an incubator. Enthusiasts experimented with mixing, dub, and talking on the mic, without always having precise information about how Jamaican sound systems actually worked.

Jo Corbeau produced the show "Ok disc-jockey" on Radio 13 starting in 1981. Tatou, a future member of Massilia Sound System, also experimented on the radio. The Jamaican model was reconstructed from records, cassettes, broadcasts, and imagination. It was then blended with Marseille slang, local humor, dub, and Occitan.

On May 20, 1984, a public session was organized in Marseille. Sevin describes it as a founding moment in the dynamic that would give rise to Massilia Sound System. This case is valuable because it shows that cultural circulation is never a photocopy. Kingston is not "imported." It is translated, tinkered with, adapted — and in doing so, a new scene is produced.

7. Sound System, Rave, Free Party: Why Do the Words Get Muddled in France?

From the 1990s onward, another history further complicates French vocabulary. In techno and free party scenes, the term "sound system" also came to designate the sound equipment itself and, by extension, the collective that builds it, transports it, and operates it. The proximity is real: walls of speakers, the power of the bass, technical self-sufficiency, collective organization, the temporary occupation of a site, and a Do It Yourself culture.

But the shared words should not erase the differences. A rave or a free party is an event. A sound system is, first, a sound apparatus, then a collective. Jamaican reggae scenes and European techno scenes have distinct genealogies, even if they have come to share practices, technologies, and, at times, imaginaries of autonomy.

From Surveillance to Criminalization

This distinction matters when discussing repression. Since the 1990s, French public authorities have specifically regulated raves and free parties. Jean-Christophe Sevin recalls that a 1995 government circular, titled "Rave parties: high-risk situations," marked a first national formalization of a policing policy targeting these gatherings.

The law of November 15, 2001 on everyday security then introduced a specific declaration regime for certain "gatherings that are exclusively festive in nature and musical in character," organized in venues not designed for that purpose. It also allowed for the seizure of equipment in certain situations. This framework was later folded into the Code de la sécurité intérieure (Internal Security Code).

In 2026, this regime was tightened further. Since August 20, gatherings meeting the relevant criteria and likely to bring together more than 250 people are subject to mandatory declaration. The law of August 18, 2026 provides for stiffer criminal penalties for organizing or holding an illegal gathering, and notably allows for the confiscation of sound equipment and, under certain conditions, of the vehicle used to transport it.

To say that governments are "shutting down sound systems" would therefore be legally too broad a claim. It is gatherings meeting the legal criteria that are targeted. But in practice, sound equipment is one of the central material objects of this control. The seizure of a system built collectively over years can durably disrupt a collective. This is also why, in public debate, the sound system sometimes ends up being conflated with the free party itself.

8. Martinique: When the Sound System Also Becomes a Place

In Martinique, circulation produces a particularly interesting transformation of the vocabulary. In her article "Musiques dancehall et utopie dans deux sociétés caribéennes, la Jamaïque et la Martinique," Mylenn Zobda-Zebina distinguishes between the Jamaican and Martinican uses of the term.

In Jamaica, she writes, a sound system is a crew of selectors who travel with their equipment and records. In Martinique, the term can also designate "the space, the hall" where the selectors' and artists' performance takes place. This shift matters: the word no longer refers only to what moves. It can refer to the place that the practice brings into being.

Zobda-Zebina further links the emergence of Martinican sound systems to the Rastafari movement. These spaces allow "brothers" and "sisters" to gather. Ital food, discussion, music, and spiritual references are shared there. The author describes venues often set up in private homes, located outside the town center, running on Friday or Saturday nights, and able to hold around two hundred to five hundred people.

These venues are genuine alternative stages. They offer a performance space to both established and beginning artists. Their promotion then relies largely on word of mouth, flyers, and posters. Far from being mere copies of a Jamaican model, they are embedded in Martinican sociability and geography.

Concrete Places: Balata, Anse Figuier, Morne-des-Esses

Press archives allow us to attach names to this geography. In 2006, a sound system is documented at the site known as Détour Bourdin, on the road from Balata to Fort-de-France. In the early 2010s, South Clash organized regular events at Anse Figuier, in Rivière-Pilote. In 2012, an event is announced at the Espace Titine, in Bon Air, Morne-des-Esses.

Beach, private space, converted hall: the forms vary. What unites them is the collective's ability to temporarily turn a place into a stage. The space doesn't always pre-exist as a cultural venue. It becomes one because a sound system, artists, an audience, and shared codes are brought to it.

Straïka D: The Sound System as an Informal School

The interview conducted with Straïka D allows us to move from general description to lived experience. The artist recounts his beginnings within Ich Madinina, created at the Cité Calmée in Rivière-Pilote, and his later ties with Indika Sound. He cites Metal Sound, MC Janick, and selector Philippe Majesty among the figures who preceded or accompanied his generation.

Straïka D remembers family-oriented sound systems, strongly marked by Rastafari culture. Vegetarian food was served there. Deejays could take the mic. He describes them as a "space of free expression." This phrase captures their function well. People came there to listen and dance, but also to learn to hold the mic, improvise, watch others, and be recognized.

His account also emphasizes resourcefulness. When Jamaican vinyl was hard to come by, young musicians didn't stop creating. With Ich Madinina, they built instrumentals using a drum machine and a keyboard. Some played the chords, others the bass or the programming. Without an official lab, an expensive studio, or a specialized school, the collective built its own tools for learning.

He also recalls that before the internet, access to Jamaican music also depended on Caribbean radio circulation: sometimes you had to pick up radio stations from Saint Lucia or Dominica. The Martinican sound system thus appears as a node within a broader regional network.

9. Guadeloupe: Sounds as Nurseries for Dancehall

Guadeloupe also developed a significant sound system culture at the turn of the 1990s. 3DOM Sound, which grew out of Freedom Sound in Basse-Terre, is documented as early as 1991. Reggae.fr cites several venues associated with its sessions: the Mahogany in Saint-Claude, the Cascade in Le Gosier, and the Coin Vert in Gourbeyre. Here again, the sound served as a venue for playing music, but also as a first stage for artists.

In the mid-1990s, Karukera Sound System took shape around Brother Jimmy and selectors Puppa Alain and Teddy. The collective became one of the major spaces of Guadeloupean reggae-dancehall and hosted a generation of artists, including Admiral T. France Télévisions still presents Brother Jimmy as one of the pioneers of sound systems in Guadeloupe.

His career makes visible a circulation that is often overlooked. His professional biography mentions experience as a deejay and MC in several sounds in mainland France between 1990 and 1995, before his work with Karukera Sound System. The movement is thus not simply "Jamaica → the Antilles." It also passes through mainland France, cassettes, radio, travel, and diasporic networks.

In an interview broadcast in 2026, Brother Jimmy also explains the choice of the name "Karukera." For him, naming the sound was a way of immediately locating its Guadeloupean identity and recalling the island's Amerindian history. This example neatly sums up the process studied here: a model circulates, but the territory that takes it in renames it and reinscribes it within its own memory.

Conclusion — Following the Wakes Rather Than a Straight Line

The history of the sound system cannot be reduced either to the history of a machine or to that of a musical genre. It is the history of a technology that became a social practice. In Kingston, the amplification of records fed an economy of dance, competition, and innovation. It went on to help transform Jamaican music production itself.

In London, the sound system accompanied Caribbean migration. It gave sonic power to forms of sociability that sometimes had to be built away from dominant spaces. With Notting Hill Carnival, it moved from the house and the club to a form of public and heritage recognition.

In mainland France, it was translated once again. Paris built its own networks. Marseille recomposed the practice around free radio. Later, techno and free party cultures appropriated the term and some of its techniques in turn, to the point of blurring, today, the genealogies in everyday and administrative language.

In Martinique and Guadeloupe, finally, sound systems became spaces of training, sociability, and creation. They settled into places that were not necessarily meant for performance. They trained artists. They connected neighborhoods, towns, islands, and diasporas. Above all, they show that a musical practice never travels intact.

What circulates are speakers, records, gestures, words, and ways of making community. At every stop, the sound system leaves a wake — and transforms in turn.

A Few Terms to Know

Sound system — Originally, sound equipment; by extension, the crew that operates it and the set of practices surrounding it.
Selector — The person who chooses and sequences the records in a sound system.
Deejay — In the Jamaican tradition, a vocal performer who talks, chants, or improvises on the mic over tracks.
Dance — In Jamaican vocabulary, can refer to the party or session itself, not only to the act of dancing.
Dubplate — An exclusive pressing or recording made for a sound system, often used as a resource of prestige or competition.
Clash — A competition between sound systems, based on selection, exclusive tracks, mic performance, and audience reaction.
Free party — An autonomous festive event, generally associated with electronic music. It is not a synonym for sound system.
