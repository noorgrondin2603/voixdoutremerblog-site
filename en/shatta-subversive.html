Is Shatta Subversive?

This is an English translation (AI-assisted, reviewed by the author) of an article originally published in French on Sillages Sonores. Read the original in French →

7 min read · Originally published December 4, 2025 · Body, language, and power in postcolonial Martinique

The question deserves to be asked head-on. When a musical genre is accused of obscenity by institutions, shunned by local media for a decade, then slowly reclaimed by streaming platforms and mainstream festivals, what exactly is happening? Is this a subversion that has been neutralized, digested by the system? Or does shatta's very trajectory — born in Martinique's working-class neighborhoods at the turn of the 2000s, ignored, scorned, then impossible to ignore — stand as proof of its political force?

To answer this, we first need to define our terms. Subversion cannot be reduced to moral provocation or sexual transgression. It is a rupture in the regimes of visibility, representation, and cultural legitimacy. Three authors help us think through this: Frantz Fanon (Black Skin, White Masks, 1952), Stuart Hall ("Cultural Identity and Diaspora," 1990), and Dick Hebdige (Subculture: The Meaning of Style, 1979). Put in dialogue with Martinican history and the concrete practices of shatta, they reveal that this genre is not mere neighborhood entertainment. It is a political apparatus, embedded in a long history of the Black body in the Antilles.

The body as first territory

Fanon put it with clinical precision: in the colonial order, the Black body is overdetermined before it even speaks. It is made legible against its will, carrying imposed signs — exaggerated sexuality, presumed violence, bodily availability. This "epidermalization of inferiority," in his phrase, locks the Black subject into a forced visibility: always seen, never the one observing. This regime of overexposure did not vanish with departmentalization in 1946, nor with the formal end of colonization. It has been carried forward in institutions, in collective imagination, in the norms of respectability imposed on Black bodies — and particularly on Black women's bodies — in postcolonial Martinican society.

It is precisely against this legacy that shatta operates. When artists like Cécinelle, Shiine, or Sage stage their bodies in whine or shake choreographies, they are not passively reproducing colonial sexualization. They are turning the stigma around. What had been judged obscene, unbecoming, unworthy of the "respectable" Antillean woman becomes a claim, a jubilation, a power. The body is no longer the object of an outside gaze that classifies and condemns it. It becomes the subject of its own staging, the author of its own pleasure.

This reversal is far from trivial in a context where the colonial school, as historian Clara Palmiste has shown, was precisely tasked with disciplining Creole girls' bodies, erasing attributes deemed too "African" or too "sensual," and forcing them into the mold of the European bourgeois woman. Female shatta short-circuits this centuries-old injunction. It refuses shame. And that is exactly why it still disturbs.

Language as a battlefield

Fanon also identified colonial language as a technology of power. Speaking Creole meant belonging to a dominated status. Speaking "proper" French meant symbolically whitening oneself, gaining temporary access to respectability. This linguistic hierarchy did not disappear after 1946. It still structures perceptions of cultural legitimacy in contemporary Martinique.

Shatta chooses Creole. Without softening it. Without translating it for an outside gaze. The lyrics mobilize Martinican Creole in the full vivacity of its oral form, with its raw imagery, its wordplay, its local references that only an inside eye can fully grasp. This is not a defensive retreat into identity. It is an affirmation: this language is enough, it doesn't need to justify itself, it doesn't need to ask permission.

Stuart Hall, in "Cultural Identity and Diaspora," offers a complementary framework for reading this gesture. Caribbean identity, for Hall, is not a stable essence but a process of permanent becoming, built through displacement, hybridity, creolization. This identity "in the making" is, by definition, productive of subversive forms, because it refuses fixed assignments. Shatta embodies exactly this logic: it absorbs Jamaican dancehall, Afro-Caribbean rhythms, references from Martinican street life, and produces from them something that did not exist before — something that escapes both the categories of local "high culture" and the circuits of international world music. This Creole hybridity is not a surface-level syncretism. It produces an autonomous aesthetic that needs no outside validation to exist.

Style as political language

Dick Hebdige, in his analysis of 1970s British subcultures, showed that style — clothing, hairstyles, gestures, attitudes, music — functions as a coded language through which marginalized groups express social resistance. This language is perceived by dominant institutions as "noise," a symbolic disturbance of order. Its subversiveness lies precisely in its visibility: it makes legible what was meant to stay invisible, it claims legitimacy for what had been relegated to the margins.

Shatta's media trajectory follows exactly this pattern. Between 2012 and 2018, the Martinican local press treated the genre with condescension or hostility: articles emphasized its "vulgar," "obscene" character, judged its lyrics disrespectful of women, worried about its influence on young people. This was the gaze of cultural institutions that did not recognize this genre as a legitimate form of expression. Between 2019 and 2022, the tone shifted without becoming clearer: a mix of fascination and repulsion, an anthropological gaze that exoticized shatta as a "sociological phenomenon" while keeping its distance. From 2022–2023 onward, the reclamation accelerated: mixed festivals, institutional playlists, more sympathetic press coverage. Shatta had become "interesting." It was being digested.

But this reclamation — which Hebdige had already analyzed in the case of punk and reggae — does not mark the death of subversion. It attests to its past effectiveness. If shatta disturbed Martinican cultural authorities this much, it is because it was actually shifting the symbolic order — the one that decides who has the right to visibility, to pleasure, to a voice in public space.

Agency, gender, and the limits of subversion

Still, we must resist too linear a reading. Subversion is not a permanent state, nor an intrinsic property of a musical genre. It plays out in specific contexts, in shifting power relations, and it carries its own contradictions.

Some shatta lyrics hold an internal tension that is hard to ignore. The genre that claims women's bodily freedom also produces tracks that objectify them, and masculine figures that reproduce relations of domination. The notion of agency, as developed by Judith Butler and in cultural studies, is indispensable here to avoid two symmetrical traps: denying any agency to the women who perform shatta on the grounds that sexualization is always alienation, or, conversely, glorifying without nuance every expression of female sexuality as an act of resistance.

Agency does not mean total freedom. It designates the space of possible action, sometimes narrow, within which a subject can bend norms, redirect signs, perform differently. A female shatta artist who seizes the sexual codes society imposes on her, to turn them to her own advantage, is not free of constraint. But she is not a mere victim of the system either. She negotiates. And it is this negotiation — always partial, always situated — that lies at the heart of the subversive dynamic.

This is also why conscious shatta and feminist shatta, categories that established themselves within the genre from around 2015–2016 onward, represent a significant shift. Artists like Ti Blica or Sorrow do more than simply turn the stigma around. They build an explicit discourse about Martinican society, its inequalities, its silences, its colonial legacies. The body remains central, but it is now articulated with political speech. Bodily subversion and discursive subversion reinforce each other.

A Caribbean laboratory

Putting Fanon, Hall, and Hebdige in dialogue around shatta is not a gratuitous academic exercise. It helps us understand why this genre, born on the margins of Martinican society, produced so much institutional resistance. Shatta touches, simultaneously, the three nerve centers of Martinique's postcolonial order: the hierarchy of bodies (Black/white, respectable/unworthy), the hierarchy of languages (legitimate French/dominated Creole), the hierarchy of cultures (exportable high culture/local popular culture).

By disturbing all three hierarchies at once, it concentrates on itself a social anxiety that goes far beyond music. The criticisms leveled at it — vulgarity, obscenity, a bad example for young people — are really criticisms leveled at everything shatta embodies: the visibility of ordinary Black bodies, Creole pride, the refusal of respectability as a condition for access to public space.

This is not the music of suffering. It is the music of those who survived suffering, and who say so with their bodies, in their own language, in their own way. That is what makes it subversive. And that is why it matters.
